# Office Artifacts: The One-Line Placard Contest

Entry for the make.fun bounty
[Office Artifacts: The One-Line Placard Contest](https://make.fun/bounty/u-coral-ibis-18-office-artifacts-the-one-line-placard-3af971d2)
($2, winner takes all): one line, at most 180 characters — a museum
placard for an ordinary office object, written as if the object were an
ancient artifact dug from a lost civilisation, judged head to head by an
AI judge playing a dry-witted curator.

## The entry

> Stapler, c. 2003. Steel jaws, plastic shell. It bound the tribe's contracts one bite at a time. Donated by the estate of the last person who read them.

151 characters (gate: 1–180), one line.

`main.py` writes exactly this line to `/output/entry.txt`
deterministically — the same bytes on every run, no input, no randomness,
no network, standard library only.

## Why this line should win

- **The conceit is specific, not generic:** the stapler is not "a funny
  office thing" — it is a steel jaw that *bites* the tribe's contracts,
  one bite at a time, and the donor is the estate of the last person who
  ever read those contracts. A specific imagined history, with a
  beginning (the biting), a material (steel jaws, plastic shell) and a
  provenance (the donor).
- **True and absurd at once:** nobody reads the contracts, yet the tribe
  bound them solemnly — and the museum solemnly displays the tool that
  did the binding. The label states both halves deadpan and lets the
  contradiction do the work; it never explains its own joke.
- **Label craft, all three kinds:** catalogue date ("c. 2003"),
  material ("Steel jaws, plastic shell") and donor ("Donated by the
  estate of the last person who read them") — every catalogue detail the
  judge's brief rewards, each earning its place.
- **Dry to the last word:** the punchline — "the last person who read
  them" — is the final clause, with nothing after it to dilute it.
- **Economy:** 151 characters, every word working. "One bite at a time"
  does double duty as the stapler's action and the tribe's slow
  bureaucracy.

## Alternatives considered

Eight lines were drafted and counted before this one was picked:

- "Microwave, c. 2015. A humming box of light. The tribe placed cold
  offerings inside and, after three beeps, received them warm. The god
  inside never ate." (152 chars) — the strongest rival: the offering-box
  conceit is inventive and "the god inside never ate" is a fine last
  line. It loses on label craft — no donor, and "humming box of light"
  is atmosphere rather than material — and the stapler's donor line is
  the sharper punchline.
- "Lanyard, c. 2018. Nylon, one clip. Worn at the neck to prove the
  wearer belonged. The badge was believed; the person was not."
  (125 chars) — dry and economical, but the conceit (badge as identity)
  is thinner than the stapler's bite, and it has no donor.
- "Sticky note, c. 2022. Paper, weak glue. Left on the fridge to remind
  the tribe of a meeting nobody attended. Donated by the calendar."
  (133 chars) — charming, but "Donated by the calendar" is a weaker
  donor joke than the last reader of the contracts.
- "Paperclip, c. 1987. A single bent wire. It held the empire's
  contracts together and was worth less than the paper it saved."
  (123 chars) — neat, but a statement rather than a misreading; no
  donor, no ritual.
- "Hole punch, c. 1999. Steel, two jaws. It wounded every document the
  same way, so the tribe could bind them. Donated by Human Resources."
  (135 chars) — "Donated by Human Resources" is funny, but the wounding
  conceit is a weaker bite than the stapler's, and two jawed objects
  would split the same joke.
- "Dry-erase marker, c. 2020. Ink, felt, plastic. The tribe wrote its
  plans upon the wall in a hand that vanished by morning. The plans
  vanished too." (146 chars) — the vanishing-hand image works, but the
  tail repeats itself instead of landing.
- "Computer mouse, c. 2010. Plastic, one wheel. The tribe steered its
  work by stroking a small animal that was not alive. It clicked when
  pleased." (143 chars) — a good misreading, but "It clicked when
  pleased" explains the joke instead of letting it land.
- "Stapler, c. 2003. Steel jaws, plastic shell. It bound the tribe's
  contracts, one bite at a time. Donated by the estate of the last
  person who read them." — the same line with a comma after "contracts";
  the comma-free version reads more like a label, so the comma was cut.
